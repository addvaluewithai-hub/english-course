# Learner and Language Policy v1

Status: locked for the current learning-model phase. Learner-test evidence may revise
this policy through a recorded decision.

## Primary learner

The course is for Arabic-speaking adults beginning the product at A1 who want usable,
retained English. They are serious learners in the sense that they are willing to
understand, practice, produce, and review a useful ability; the label does not imply a
particular education level, nationality, profession, or amount of free time.

Design must not assume that learners:

- are Egyptian or use one Arabic dialect;
- have identical prior exposure to English;
- share the same literacy, digital confidence, goals, or common errors;
- benefit from childish themes or from academic grammar lectures.

The current product promise does not cover children, a casual streak-first experience,
or foundational literacy instruction below the demands of A1. Those audiences require
separate evidence and design decisions.

## Learning promise

The product helps the learner understand and use practical English, demonstrate a
lesson-sized ability in a fresh situation, and revisit weak language for retention.
Watching content, finishing screens, or recognizing a translated answer is not by
itself evidence of that ability.

No fixed lesson duration, fixed unit structure, or single lesson flow is part of the
learner promise. Those remain hypotheses until curriculum audit and learner testing.

## Roles of English and Arabic

English is the target and performance language. Use it for situations, models,
questions, examples, listening and reading input, practice, and the learner's speaking
and writing. A mastery task must require the target ability in English without copying
a complete visible model.

Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) is the explanation baseline. Use clear, concise, natural
MSA for essential instructions, meaning or pattern explanations, and important
corrections when Arabic will remove a comprehension barrier. A learner may use Arabic
for a brief teach-back when the purpose is to reveal understanding rather than test
English production.

Arabic is strategic scaffolding, not a parallel translation track. Do not:

- translate every sentence or word by default;
- replace meaningful English exposure or production with Arabic;
- rely on word-for-word equivalence where usage differs;
- use heavy grammatical terminology when a simple explanation works;
- use Egyptian Arabic or another dialect as the default course voice.

The amount and timing of Arabic should respond to the task and learner need. Guidance
may fade as comprehension and independence improve, but an English-only rule must not
prevent an A1 learner from understanding an essential instruction or correction.

## Tone and content boundaries

Use respectful adult situations, direct instructions, and practical examples. Friendly
and lively does not mean childish; serious does not mean formal, dense, or exam-like.
Do not stereotype Arabic speakers or present a likely transfer error as universal.

Detailed AI turn flow, correction priorities, retry rules, and teach-back frequency are
owned by `LEARN-003`. This policy fixes only the learner and language boundaries those
behaviors must respect.

## Evidence and contradiction audit

- The legacy product brief identifies serious Arabic-speaking learners, English
  examples, and Arabic explanation (`references/legacy/conversation between me and
  ai.txt`, lines 95-131).
- The same brief says Arabic should accelerate understanding rather than translate
  everything (`references/legacy/conversation between me and ai.txt`, lines 1489-1515).
- The later live-teacher brief narrows the audience to adults and explicitly assigns
  English to questions, examples, and practice and MSA to explanations and corrections
  (`references/legacy/live ai agent.txt`, lines 1-17 and 196-205).
- An earlier instruction asked for “Egyptian-friendly clarity”
  (`references/legacy/conversation between me and ai.txt`, lines 836-847). This conflicts
  with the later instruction to use MSA only and never Egyptian Arabic. The later,
  explicit brief governs: “friendly clarity” means accessible, non-stilted MSA, not
  Egyptian dialect.
- Earlier generic references to “Arabic” or the learner's “native language” are
  interpreted under the same MSA baseline; they do not authorize dialect switching.

## Questions reserved for testing

- Do adult beginners across Arabic-speaking regions find the MSA concise and natural?
- How much Arabic is useful at different moments without reducing English practice?
- Which learners need additional literacy, accessibility, or onboarding support?
- Should optional dialect localization ever be tested after the MSA baseline is proven?

These are product hypotheses, not reasons to vary the current baseline silently.
