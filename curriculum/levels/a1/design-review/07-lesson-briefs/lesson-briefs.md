## Instructional-load rule

A source record being introduced in a lesson does **not** mean every record becomes a separate explicit teaching objective.

Each lesson distinguishes:

- **Foreground language/performance** — actively noticed, practised, and needed for the lesson task.
- **Embedded introduction/support** — encountered meaningfully in models, texts, examples, images, instructions, or contrast sets; it remains required source coverage but is not independently drilled just because it has its own source record.
- **Recycle** — previously encountered language used again under a changed prompt/context.
- **Evidence task** — observable learner performance that determines whether the lesson objective was achieved.

This distinction is necessary because several EGP features or ability descriptors can describe one learner performance, and a lexical set can contain many words that are naturally learned together. The exact ledger remains responsible for proving that no source record disappears and that every required record receives later retrieval/verification.

## Authoring guardrails

1. One clear primary learner performance per instructional lesson.
2. Do not turn every source item into a slide, definition, or isolated drill.
3. Chunks come before unnecessary grammar explanation.
4. Grammar explanation is bounded to what the learner needs to perform the task.
5. A lexical set may be broad, but only a manageable core is foregrounded at once; the rest may enter through supported recognition/context and become more active through later reuse.
6. Pronunciation is attached to language the learner is actually using.
7. Unit-end transfer lessons teach no hidden new system; they change the prompt/context and require retrieval.
8. A complete model may support learning earlier in a lesson, but mastery evidence may not be copied from a complete model.
9. Support may clarify the task or individual language, but may not supply the learner's final response.
10. If actual authoring reveals that a brief cannot fit without rushing or superficial exposure, split the lesson and update the 62-slot architecture rather than compressing it artificially.

# Unit 1 — First Contact: Me and You

Unit purpose: give a new A1 learner enough language to survive a first social encounter, exchange core identity/contact information, handle basic numbers and spelling repair, and complete/use a simple personal profile.

Primary capabilities: **C01, C02, C11, C17, C20**.  
Main cross-cutting requirements: **X03, X04, X05, X06, X07, X10**.  
Unit-end transfer: **U1-L07**.

## U1-L01 — Hello. I'm …

### Primary performance
Open a very simple first-contact exchange, exchange names, ask/answer basic wellbeing, and close the first turn politely.

### End-of-lesson evidence
With a new identity/name prompt and no complete script, the learner can:
- greet another person;
- say their name;
- ask the other person's name;
- understand/respond to `How are you?` with a simple answer;
- use one basic polite response.

Minor pauses and model-supported pronunciation are acceptable at this point.

### Foreground language
Core chunks/frames:
- `Hello / Hi.`
- `Good morning.`
- `I'm …`
- `My name is …`
- `What's your name?`
- `How are you?`
- `I'm fine / pretty good / really good.`
- `Thank you / Thanks.`

Oxford Phrase List foreground from this slot:
- `Good morning`
- `How are you?`
- `pretty good`
- `really good`
- `Thank you`

### Grammar foreground
Use, rather than lecture, the minimum identity/question system needed here:
- first-/second-person reference;
- linking `be` in short identity/wellbeing statements;
- simple `be` question/answer frames;
- short affirmative responses.

The ledger contains multiple EGP records here because these forms have several structural descriptions. They are **not eight separate grammar mini-lessons**.

### Lexical foreground
Prioritise the words that make the encounter work: name, hello/hi, morning, good/fine, thank/thanks, person/people where useful.

Other source words assigned to this lesson may appear inside supported examples or recognition material; do not force all 13 lexical records into isolated recall drills.

### Pronunciation
- recognise names and short learnt chunks in very clear speech;
- basic word stress in names;
- recognisable greeting/question contour;
- intelligibility over accent imitation.

### Learning sequence
1. Comprehensible first encounter with visual/context support.
2. Notice the few reusable chunks above.
3. Controlled substitution of names and wellbeing responses.
4. Short listening discrimination with different speakers/names.
5. Guided exchange without reading a full dialogue.
6. New-name mini exchange as lesson evidence.

### Planned retrieval
- new-partner greeting in U1-L03;
- introduction/reacting language expands in U1-L06;
- fresh integrated personal exchange in U1-L07.

### Do not do
- do not teach the news/reaction cluster here (`bad news`, `Guess what…`, `Oh no!`, etc.); it was deliberately moved to U1-L06 after human load review;
- do not explain the whole `be` system;
- do not require a memorised full dialogue as evidence.

### Ledger load after human critic
**8 abilities / 5 phrases / 8 grammar records / 13 word records / 2 pronunciation records = 36 source introductions.**  
Interpretation: 36 traceability records, **not** 36 equal explicit targets.

## U1-L02 — How old are you?

### Primary performance
Give and understand age and basic functional numbers in a personal-information exchange.

### End-of-lesson evidence
Given unfamiliar age/number prompts, the learner can understand and say the required basic numbers and exchange age using a simple question/answer frame.

### Foreground language
- basic number words/ranges required by the source evidence;
- `How old are you?`
- `I'm … years old.` / `X years old`.

### Phrase decision after human critic
`millions of`, `thousands of`, and `more than…` were removed from this lesson. They belong to the A1 inventory but do not naturally serve an age lesson; they now enter in U5-L04 with quantity language.

### Grammar foreground
Only the form needed for age questions/answers and number phrases. Do not build a general question-system lecture here.

### Lexical handling
The source ledger introduces 21 word records here, many of them number forms. Teach the number system as patterns and meaningful number reading/listening, not as 21 unrelated vocabulary flashcards.

### Pronunciation
- number contrast recognition;
- stress/syllable clarity in easily confused number forms;
- intelligible age response.

### Learning sequence
1. Hear/identify numbers in personal contexts.
2. Pattern the number system rather than serial memorisation only.
3. Ask/answer age with changing profiles.
4. Short listening with distractor numbers.
5. Fresh age-card exchange.

### Planned retrieval
- number strings in U1-L05 contact details;
- personal-profile transfer U1-L07;
- quantities/prices in Unit 5;
- ticket information in Unit 6.

### Ledger load
**4 abilities / 1 phrase / 2 grammar records / 21 word records / 1 pronunciation record = 29 source introductions.**

## U1-L03 — Where are you from?

### Primary performance
Say and understand basic origin/residence information and initiate a simple question about it.

### End-of-lesson evidence
With a fresh person/location card, the learner can ask where someone is from, state where they are from/live using the taught bounded frames, and understand a short clear answer.

### Foreground language
- `Where are you from?`
- `I'm from …`
- `I live in …`
- `come from…`
- country / city / place language needed by the profiles.

### Recycle from earlier lessons
- greeting + name from U1-L01;
- age/number language where a profile includes age.

The encounter should now become: greet → name → origin/residence, rather than a disconnected geography drill.

### Grammar foreground
- basic `where` question frame;
- `be + from` identity/origin pattern;
- simple subject + verb/place pattern where required for `live`;
- relevant personal reference only.

### Lexical handling
Twenty source word records are assigned here. Foreground the small set needed to run the exchange; use the remainder through varied country/city/person profiles rather than a decontextualised list.

### Pronunciation
- country/city/name word stress where lesson-critical;
- clear short question contour;
- recognise place names in slow, clear input.

### Learning sequence
1. Read/hear mini profiles.
2. Notice origin/residence frames.
3. Controlled question-answer swaps.
4. Information-gap profiles with different locations.
5. Fresh new-partner exchange including U1-L01 language.

### Planned retrieval
- U1-L06 introducing another person;
- Unit 2 person/family profiles;
- U6 hotel/travel details;
- U10 personal-world integration.

### Ledger load
**8 abilities / 1 phrase / 4 grammar records / 20 word records / 2 pronunciation records = 35 source introductions.**

## U1-L04 — What do you do?

### Primary performance
Give and understand very basic job/study information as part of a personal profile.

### End-of-lesson evidence
Given a fresh profile/person image, the learner can state a basic job/student fact and understand the same kind of fact from another person.

### Foreground language
- `I'm a …`
- `I work as …`
- `I work for …` where the context is transparent and natural;
- student/work/job language.

Oxford phrases introduced here:
- `work as sth`
- `work for sb/sth`

### Grammar approach
No new grammar chapter is needed. Reuse identity structures already encountered and attach `work` frames as chunks/patterns.

### Lexical handling
This slot contains 23 word records because the A1 occupation/work field is broad. Do **not** demand instant active recall of 20+ occupations in one pass.

Use three layers:
- foreground a practical core of high-value role words plus `job/work/student`;
- supported recognition/sorting for the wider occupation set;
- require later retrieval when people/jobs recur in Unit 2 and elsewhere.

A learner should leave able to exchange a job/study fact, not merely recite an occupation list.

### Learning sequence
1. Match people/images to familiar role words.
2. Hear/read micro-profiles.
3. Build `I'm a… / I work as…` statements.
4. Ask/respond using a bounded job/study question frame.
5. Fresh profile identification/exchange.

### Planned retrieval
- U1-L06 third-person introduction;
- Unit 2 familiar-person descriptions;
- Unit 4 work/study routine;
- Unit 7 past life/work facts.

### Ledger load
**5 abilities / 2 phrases / 0 new grammar records / 23 word records = 30 source introductions.**

## U1-L05 — Can you spell that?

### Primary performance
Give basic contact details and repair a communication problem by asking for spelling/written form.

### End-of-lesson evidence
In a fresh contact-detail exchange, the learner can give a basic phone/email/address field, understand a partner's key detail, and use a repair strategy when a name or word is unclear.

### Foreground language
Contact information:
- phone / phone number;
- email;
- address;
- basic number strings.

Source phrase cluster:
- `cell phone`
- `mobile phone`
- `phone number`

Useful embedded/reference phrases:
- `on the phone`
- `phone call`

Required authored repair language because the ability source requires the function even though the Oxford A1 Phrase List does not supply a dedicated repair phrase:
- `How do you spell that?`
- `Can you spell it?`
- `Can you write it down?`
- simple repetition/checking language as needed.

### Grammar approach
Keep grammar subordinate to the repair/contact task. The lesson is not a generic modal-question lesson.

### Writing/mechanics
This is the first explicit functional spelling/writing thread:
- common names;
- letters/digits in contact fields;
- basic capitalisation where required;
- accurate enough transcription to preserve the information.

### Pronunciation
- letter/name/number intelligibility;
- monitoring and repair;
- pausing safely and repeating/spelling a key item.

### Learning sequence
1. Hear/read a contact card.
2. Exchange one field at a time.
3. Introduce a deliberate unclear-name moment.
4. Teach and practise repair chunks because the need is now real.
5. Information gap with unseen contact details.
6. Fresh exchange where repair may or may not be necessary.

### Planned retrieval
- U1-L07 new personal-profile task;
- U6-L06 hotel/travel details;
- continuous repair behaviour across later speaking tasks;
- exit evidence includes repair opportunity.

### Ledger load
**7 abilities / 5 phrases / 1 grammar record / 22 word records / 1 pronunciation record = 36 source introductions.**

## U1-L06 — This is my friend …

### Primary performance
Introduce another person, respond socially to an introduction/simple piece of news, and close the encounter.

### Why this lesson carries the wider social phrase cluster
Human critic moved the reaction/news phrases out of U1-L01. They fit this broader social-response lesson much better and keep the first lesson focused.

### End-of-lesson evidence
Using a fresh person card and short social cue, the learner can:
- introduce another person;
- understand/respond to the introduction;
- give one or two basic facts about that person using already learned language;
- react appropriately to one simple positive/negative cue;
- close the encounter.

### Foreground language
Introduction/reference:
- `This is …`
- `he / she / him` and basic person reference as source-supported;
- `someone else`
- `speak to sb`
- `See you`

Social reaction cluster, introduced here deliberately:
- `good news / bad news`
- `Guess what…`
- `Oh yes / Oh no!`
- `I'm sorry`
- `welcome to sth`
- `You're welcome`

Not all twelve source phrases need twelve separate drills. Teach them as a small number of **interaction routines**:
1. introduce/respond;
2. react to simple news;
3. welcome/polite response;
4. leave-taking.

### Recycle
- names/wellbeing from L01;
- origin from L03;
- job/study from L04.

The learner should now combine earlier personal-information pieces in third-person social use.

### Grammar foreground
- basic third-person reference sufficient for the introduction;
- linking `be`/short statements as needed;
- no full pronoun-system lecture.

### Lexical handling
22 word records enter here, but many support the social routines or third-person profile. Foreground the words required by those routines; wider person/social vocabulary can be recognised in the short profiles and strengthened in Unit 2.

### Learning sequence
1. Model a short introduction with one social response.
2. Notice third-person reference.
3. Introduce people from changing cards.
4. Add one simple fact from L03/L04.
5. Reaction mini-scenarios: positive / negative / welcome.
6. Close naturally with a short leave-taking.
7. Fresh social encounter without a full written script.

### Planned retrieval
- immediate integrated transfer U1-L07;
- Unit 2 person/family work;
- later personal messages/social language;
- A1 exit personal-world evidence.

### Ledger load
**4 abilities / 12 phrases / 2 grammar records / 22 word records = 40 source introductions.**

This is the densest Unit-1 social slot. Authoring must preserve the four-routine grouping above. If the actual lesson requires twelve independent chunk drills, the brief has failed and the slot must be split rather than rushed.

## U1-L07 — Who is this person? — Unit 1 Transfer

### Primary performance
Complete and use a **new** personal profile by integrating Unit-1 language rather than repeating any one lesson model.

### Transfer design
Learners receive incomplete/new information, not the same examples used in instruction. The task should require a combination of:
- greeting/opening;
- name;
- age/basic number information;
- origin/residence;
- job/study;
- contact detail or spelling repair;
- form/profile completion;
- introducing or reporting a person where appropriate.

### Required evidence modes
Use at least two modalities so this is not just a speaking role-play. A strong bundle is:
1. read/listen for missing personal details;
2. ask a partner/system for remaining information;
3. complete a simple profile/form;
4. use the completed information in a short unscripted exchange/introduction.

### New-source introduction boundary
This lesson should contain essentially **no hidden new grammar or vocabulary system**. The ledger currently introduces only one ability record and the phrase `find out`; `find out` can function naturally in task framing/instructions and later use, but must not turn the transfer lesson into a new phrase-teaching chapter.

### Success criteria
The learner succeeds if they can complete the new task with understandable A1 language while:
- retrieving rather than copying a complete model;
- initiating at least one relevant question;
- understanding/responding to basic questions;
- repairing at least one communication problem if the task creates one;
- recording personal information accurately enough for the purpose.

### What this lesson verifies
Fresh Unit-1 evidence for **C01, C02, C11, C17, C20**, with X03/X04/X05/X06/X07/X10 conditions as appropriate.

### Planned later recycle
- Unit 2 expands from self to family/familiar people;
- Unit 5 reuses numbers/questions in transactions;
- Unit 6 reuses personal/contact details in travel/hotel tasks;
- Unit 10 tests personal-world transfer again under new prompts.

### Ledger load
**1 ability / 1 phrase / 0 grammar / 0 new word / 0 pronunciation = 2 source introductions.**  
The low introduction count is intentional: this is evidence/retrieval, not another teaching dump.

# Unit 1 manual critic — applied before lock

The following issues were found and fixed while converting the clean source ledger into actual lesson briefs:

1. **U1-L01 social overload:** reaction/news phrases did not belong in a first name/wellbeing exchange. They were moved to U1-L06. U1-L01 phrase introductions dropped from 13 to 5.
2. **U1-L02 number-context mismatch:** `millions of`, `thousands of`, and `more than…` were valid A1 phrases but unnatural in an age lesson. They were moved to U5-L04 quantities. U1-L02 now has one phrase target: `X years old`.
3. **Source-record count ≠ teaching-object count:** multiple ability/grammar records often describe the same observable performance. The brief now explicitly prevents one-record-one-drill authoring.
4. **Occupation lexical density:** U1-L04 must use active core + supported recognition + later retrieval, not 23 isolated vocabulary cards.
5. **U1-L06 phrase density:** 12 phrases are accepted only because they form four coherent social routines. Actual lesson authoring must split the slot if those routines cannot be taught/practised naturally within the product's lesson format.
6. **Transfer integrity:** U1-L07 remains almost entirely new-content-free and therefore functions as a real fresh-retrieval checkpoint.
